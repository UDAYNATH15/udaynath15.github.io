Positioning map

Each dot is a competitor: its price across, its store reviews up (log scale). The gold line is your price.
The economics you're using: games that players see as close substitutes compete for the same time and money, so the more of them there are, the more price-elastic demand for yours becomes — raise your price and players switch. Digital games have low barriers to entry (cheap tools, near-zero marginal cost, open storefronts), which is why popular genres fill up fast. The way out is product differentiation: give players a reason your game has no close substitute.
How the verdict works

The saturation score (0–100) blends three signals from the first five games you log: how fast you found them (50% — two minutes or less scores highest, thirty minutes or more lowest), how close they are to your idea (35%), and how many launched in the last three years (15%). Below 40 reads as an open niche, 40–65 as competitive, above 65 as crowded. It is a rule of thumb for thinking, not market research — your own cited sources always win.

About this project

This toolkit is a semester-long build for EconS 329 — The Economics of Gaming at Washington State University. Each week, one feature is added and published here. The goal: give students a fast way to test the numbers behind their game proposals — market, budget, break-even, and net present value — against the same criteria the course uses.

It is optional and ungraded. Nothing here is submitted; the official course lives in Canvas. Numbers produced by the simulator are teaching estimates, not market research — your own cited sources always win.

Follow the build: the commit history shows every change, week by week.
